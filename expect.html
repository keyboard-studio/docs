A leaflet for keyboard authors

Keyboard Studio — What to Expect

You know your language. Keyboard Studio handles the machinery. Here is what making a keyboard feels like today, what is still rough, and what is on its way — in plain language.

  • Runs in your browser
  • No code to write
  • Free to use
  • English + French today
  • Works on a phone screen

Who this is for

People who know a language's alphabet and sounds — teachers, translators, community members, language workers — who have never written keyboard software, and should never need to. If that is you, this page is your map of the journey: what the studio can already do for you, where the path is still rough, and what is coming next.

The ten stops

1

Start where your language lives

What it means for you: you begin from something close to your language — never from an empty page.

Ready today
  • Begin from a keyboard close to your language — any published Keyman keyboard can be your starting point — or from plain US English if nothing close exists yet.
  • Type your language's name and the studio fills in the rest: its script, its own name for itself, and its language code.
  • Paste a paragraph of your language and your alphabet is proposed for you, drawn from large public collections of language data (known as CLDR and SLDR) that together cover thousands of languages.
  • Every character shows where it came from — your text, the language data, or the starting keyboard — and you confirm or correct the list before anything is built on it.
2

Answer questions, not code

What it means for you: the studio interviews you about your language, in plain words, one small step at a time.

Ready today
  • A step-by-step survey in plain language about your characters and how they behave — no technical vocabulary, nothing to memorise.
  • Every question arrives already filled in with a sensible proposal. You confirm it or change it — you never start from a blank page.
  • Change your mind freely. If you switch your starting keyboard later, your answers are kept and re-checked against the new start — never silently thrown away.
3

See it, tap it, try it

What it means for you: instead of reading descriptions, you try real little keyboards made from your own letters.

Ready today
  • Live mini-keyboards, built from your letters. Where a behaviour can be shown, you tap the pattern that behaves the way your language should — you don't have to imagine it from a description.
  • The studio rebuilds and checks your keyboard after every answer. It stops you only when something would actually break — not for harmless choices.
  • Problems are flagged at the exact step where you can fix them, in words about your language, where you are standing when you read them.
4

Accents & marks, handled with care

What it means for you: the fiddly part of most alphabets gets its own careful step, with your letters in the examples.

Ready today
  • A dedicated “Accents & marks” step sits right after your alphabet, while the letters are fresh in your mind.
  • Decide for each mark whether it gets its own key or combines with the letter before it — and whether it is typed before or after its letter. Both answers can live in one alphabet: a busy tone mark on its own key, and the two most common combined letters on keys of their own.
  • Interactive demos use your letters, not toy examples, so what you tap is what your community will type.
  • You also choose how backspace behaves with combined characters — peel one piece at a time, or remove the whole character.
  • The studio tells the truth about what your keyboard can type — on phone keyboards included, where marks live on keys like ◌̀.
5

Your keyboard, on phones too

What it means for you: the keyboard you design is the keyboard your community gets on their phones — not an afterthought.

Ready today
  • The touch layout for phones is shaped from the keyboard you just designed — never a fixed template that ignores your work.
  • Your choice: keep the starting keyboard's phone layout, or rebuild it from your desktop design.
  • The studio itself works on a phone-sized screen, so you can build or adjust a keyboard from the same device your community uses.
  • The studio's own screens speak English and French today, with more languages prepared through community translation.
6

Take it home

What it means for you: a finished, installable keyboard for your community — and your projects kept safe in one place.

Ready today
  • Download an installable keyboard package for your community, or the full source files if you want them.
  • Every package arrives complete — a readme, a licence, a history, a welcome page and a help page, generated for you — and you preview them before you download.
  • “My keyboards” keeps every project in one place. Your drafts are saved as you work and survive closing the browser; come back tomorrow and resume where you left off.
  • A keyboard you have shared is marked as shared, with its link kept, so you always know what the world can already see.
7

Still rough

What it means for you: the honest list. If your language or your plan touches one of these, better to know before you start.

Watch out
  • Scripts: the proven path covers the Latin, Cyrillic, Greek, Georgian and Armenian alphabetic scripts. Ethiopic, Chinese, Japanese and Korean writing systems are not supported yet.
  • Copying an existing keyboard can still lose some polish — a font or a welcome picture may go missing from the copy. The team is fixing these right now; check your package's welcome page and fonts before you share it.
  • There is no built-in tutorial and no finished user manual yet. This page is the guide for now — and the studio's questions are written to be answered without one.
  • The studio is young. Expect it to change: screens may move, and new stops will appear on this map as they open.
8

On its way to you

What it means for you: things being built now that will make your keyboard more fully yours.

On its way
  • Write your keyboard's welcome and help text inside the studio, in your own words, instead of accepting generated pages.
  • Design your keyboard's icon — the little picture your community will recognise it by.
  • Bring a keyboard you already have as source files and adapt it, even if it was never published anywhere.
  • A guided first-time walkthrough, for authors opening the studio for the very first time.
  • Better preview fonts, including Andika, and coverage for scripts the standard fonts miss — so what you see while designing is what your letters really look like.
  • For confident authors, an assisted editor for fine-tuning the rules directly — still without raw code.
9

The last mile — your keyboard, worldwide

⚑ The summit of this map: your keyboard, in the official Keyman library, where anyone in the world can find and install it.

Partly ready

Ready in part

The studio can already prepare a submission to the Keyman library — the collection where Keyman keyboards are shared with the world — packaged the way the library expects.

Not yet

No studio-made keyboard has completed the library's review yet, and review questions today expect a technical author. So the project has ruled that a named human shepherd will walk each submission through review with its author. Conversations with the Keyman team begin in November 2026.

The promise: you will not face the review process alone, and you will never be asked to learn Git.

10

What we promise

What it means for you: the rules the studio works by, stated plainly so you can hold us to them.

Our word
  • “Defaults are proposals.”

    The studio suggests; you decide. Nothing it fills in for you is final until you have seen it and let it stand.

  • “Nothing silent.”

    If the studio changes its mind about something of yours, it asks — showing you the change — rather than quietly rewriting your work.

  • “Warnings live where you can act.”

    A problem is flagged at the step that owns it, in plain words — not collected into a scary list somewhere else.

  • “Your work is yours.”

    Drafts stay until you delete them. And a copy never steals the original author's words as your description — your keyboard's story is yours to write.

  • “Your data has a named keeper.”

    The studio's privacy notice names SIL Global as responsible for personal data — and reporting a crash never includes your text unless the design says so.

Requests and ideas: they land as issues on the project's GitHub — there is no separate feedback forum. That is where an ask arrives, and where its landing is recorded.
